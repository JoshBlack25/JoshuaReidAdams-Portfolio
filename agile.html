Charter v2.0 (Oct 2026) Original charter v1.0 (May 2026)

When I took over as Team Lead in July, my responsibilities did not change much on paper. What changed was my mindset: I had to think like a development lead. I divided the first frontend work using the commuter navigation bar from our wireframes, so each member built a section of it. Joshua Bird joined about a month later, by which time the commuter frontend was basically done.

In practice, the project concept and the design choices I worked out with the team became our charter. They guided what I implemented and what I asked others to implement day to day. Everyone knew what GoldenWay had to do for the commuter and how the screens should look and behave, so I could bring decisions back to that shared picture rather than to the written document from semester 1.

The working agreement I relied on most was that nobody pushes directly to main. I was in charge of version control. I had everyone work on their own branch and push from their forked repository, and I merged those branches into main myself, checking the code and resolving any conflicts. I also asked that anyone changing a file another member was working on let that person know first.

Delegating work mostly felt manageable, but as lead I sometimes had to make tough decisions about who did what. The team was accommodating, and we delivered the areas we had committed to.

If I started again, I would write our full tech stack into the charter from day one, from the frontend to the backend, including everything I planned to add. I would also record design choices and working agreements in the charter as I made them, so the written version always matches how the team actually works. With the agreement clear and current, it is easier to see what has not been addressed yet. That is why I updated our charter to version 2.0, to capture the concept, roles and agreements that had guided us.

How a story became tasks

Each story was split into tasks on a sprint card checklist. For example, "As a commuter, I want to tap to board" became: build the route picker, write the Supabase RPC that deducts a journey, apply the transfer window rule, record the trip in ride history, and show the updated balance.

Commuter stories on TrelloStaff-role stories on Trello

Our first user stories were written only for the commuter. I proposed turning GoldenWay into a multirole system, with staff users whose job is to help the commuter on their journey: clerks, inspectors, agents and the admin. Writing stories for those roles from the commuter's point of view made the idea feel solid to me, because every new feature had a clear reason to exist. It also gave the app room to grow and gave each team member, including our new member, a clear area to focus on.

Not every story made it into the app in its original form. I wanted the QR code story to run through several parts of the system. For example, a commuter would receive a ticket receipt with a QR code that the inspector could scan. Once development started, I realised how much of the app that one feature would touch, and with the time we had it could not be built the way I wanted. Instead, inspector verification was built around the Gold Card itself. The inspector looks up the card when the commuter taps on and sees whether it has a valid pass or journeys left. That kept the user need, proving that the commuter has paid, and matched how a real GABS card already works.

The way I broke stories into tasks also changed during the project. I first built the commuter frontend from our wireframes, which gave me a strong base. When the team evaluated it, I went back to the drawing board and built the backend in Supabase to support that frontend. I then removed the mock data so every screen reads real data from the database. Once the commuter frontend and backend were connected, adding the five staff roles was much easier. Throughout this, the Trello board and the business rules guided which tasks came next.

Some stories were adapted to our resources. There was no budget for NFC or IoT devices, so I built tap-to-pay as a simulated validator: the commuter picks the route they are boarding and taps in the app, and the backend deducts a journey exactly as a real validator would. For a student demo application, I think the trade-off was small. My research showed that real NFC would mostly mean connecting the same flow to a device API. I also applied real validation rules. South African ID numbers are checked with the Luhn algorithm, and payments only accept valid card details, with the card number passing a Luhn check. A PayFast sandbox would have been my next step for payments.

I learned that a user story is the start of a conversation, not a fixed specification. Where a story could not be built as written, I kept the user's real need and built the closest version our time and resources allowed, based on how commuters and staff would use the app in real life.

View the product backlog on Trello

When the team first showed our Trello board to the lecturer, she was not happy with how we used it. Most of our information sat in long descriptions, and we were not really using cards and lists such as User Stories and In Progress to show the work. I led the rework with the team: we restructured the board around proper lists, gave each sprint card its own subtasks, added colour labels to group the work and removed cards we did not need. After those changes she approved the layout, and that structure became the backlog I managed for the rest of the project.

My priorities were shaped largely by our presentations to the lecturer, who acted as our customer. Each check-in had a clear focus. First the team presented our system architecture and brand design, then the frontend design as a mock-up without a backend, and later a version where the frontend and backend worked together. After each presentation I knew what she expected to see next, and I moved those items to the top of the backlog. For example, she expected authentication to be complete, so at the following check-in I showed email verification running through the SMTP setup and a password reset that lets a user recover their account by email.

The biggest change to the backlog came from my own work on the use cases. While thinking about how routes would be managed, I asked who would create and maintain them. I also considered a commuter who had already bought a Gold Card at a clerk and paid the card fee before signing up online. Questions like these showed me that the commuter app was only one side of what Golden Arrow does, and that a whole operation runs behind it. That led me to design the multirole system, with separate sign-up paths for commuters, staff and existing cardholders. I based these use cases on how Golden Arrow works and on my own online research, and they grew the app well beyond its first scope.

By feature freeze, I brought the backlog fully in line with what had been built. Where an item had changed during development, such as tap-to-pay becoming a simulated validator, I refined it to describe what was delivered.

This taught me that a product backlog is a living list. Its order should follow what the customer needs to see next, and it is most useful when it always reflects the real state of the product.

I usually ran sprint planning on a Tuesday. Each member showed me what they had built from the work I had assigned to them, and I acted as a critic of that work. I gave suggestions on what to improve, what to implement next and what to leave out, so that their next tasks were clear. When I had my own work or updates to share, I presented them to the group in the same meeting.

Getting everyone together was not always possible, because some members were busy during the day and others in the evening. Most meetings included the whole team, but where circumstances made that difficult I held one-on-one meetings instead, so each member could show me their work at a time that suited them. This kept every sprint moving even when the full group could not meet.

The most challenging part of each sprint for me was merging everyone's work. As I merged branches one after another, I had to find where each change sat in the code and decide which code to keep and which to discard: accept the incoming change, keep the current one, or combine them. I used the tools available to me to work through these conflicts in our pull requests, and the experience taught me a lot about version control.

The lecturer's feedback shaped each sprint I planned. Because of breaks during the term, there were stretches where we could not present to her, so her main concern was whether we were on track. With each presentation she saw that what she had asked for at the previous one was now built. The app moved from a mock frontend to one connected to a live database, then I showed authentication working through the SMTP setup, and payments that read and write real data instead of mock data. As the core features became stable, my own focus shifted from building features to designing new ones, and that is when I developed the multirole system.

On 21 August I wrote that I would rather show fewer features that are solid than many that are shaky. At that point some features worked fully with the backend, while others, such as the map, still used mock data. Some screens also needed UI fixes, like two different buttons leading to the same place. Making the working features solid before adding more became a principle I kept for the rest of the project.

Full logbook (PDF)

Some days I wrote my logbook entry on the day itself, and other times I had to catch up as I went along. That taught me how important it is to log my work every day, because each daily entry adds up to the picture of what I achieved that week. Logging works best when it is part of my daily routine rather than something I do afterwards.

Reading back through my entries showed me how far the project progressed, from wireframes and a mock frontend in July to a deployed app with six roles in October. The logbook also showed me where I needed to look back and review the week, and it worked as a mental checklist for what I had done and what still needed attention.

Every retrospective gave me a lesson I could act on. I learned to apply migrations to the live database before testing, and to merge branches into main one at a time. I also kept reminding the team to update their forked repositories regularly, because several of us were updating main at the same time. Keeping each fork in sync meant that the work each member sent to my main repository already included everyone else's latest changes, so our collaboration showed in the code itself. Above all, the retrospectives reminded me to stick to the plans I had set in motion.

Connect

What I take into the workplace

Looking at the charter, user stories, backlogs and logbook together, the main lesson I take into the workplace is the value of routine. Before I write any code, I update my branches and make sure I understand what I need to do, whether as team lead or as developer. I document as I go, so the backlog stays current and anyone can see what changed, when it changed and why I chose that approach. I lay the foundation before development starts: documentation, system architecture, brand guide and business rules. That way less changes later, and anything that does change is recorded.

Communication matters just as much. Even on a solo project some communication has to happen, and in a team it means everyone knows what to do and what is happening right now. I need to know the resources I will need to finish a project. After a break, I do an internal evaluation of where the project stands before carrying on. As a lead, I make myself available, hold regular check-ins, and look for ways to make the work convenient for my team and to ease the strain on them.
